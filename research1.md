# Market Analysis: Separating the "Real" Indie Game Market from Low-Effort and AI-Generated Noise on Steam

## Market Size: The Illusion of Gross Volume vs. Net Commercial Reality

Steam, arguably the largest distribution platform in the PC gaming market, has witnessed an unprecedented inflation of content in recent years. The transition from older curation systems like "Steam Greenlight" to the "Steam Direct" model—where anyone can publish a game for a fee—has virtually eliminated market entry barriers. This shift created a massive bloat in the number of games on the platform, rendering the "total number of games released" metric highly unreliable for market analysis.

Data indicates that the gross growth within the Steam ecosystem is staggering. While 9,647 games were released on the platform in 2020, this figure surged to 18,945 in 2024, and 20,204 in 2025. Projections for 2026 suggest that the 24,000 mark will be crossed. However, this massive annual release volume of 18,000 to 23,000 games is far from reflecting the size of the genuine, commercially competitive indie game market. The overwhelming majority of these gross figures consists of hobby projects, asset flips, and, more recently, shovelware automatically generated by generative AI (GenAI).

To determine the net size of the actual indie game market, the "Profile Features Limited" or "Steam is learning about this game" tags—implemented by Valve to protect its own ecosystem—serve as the most critical analytical filter. To prevent products with no commercial validity from abusing the platform for trading card or achievement mining, Valve restricts the profile features of games that fail to pass a specific sales and player engagement threshold. In 2024, approximately 10,400 of the 18,945 games released (around 55%) fell directly into this restricted category. When these low-effort projects are filtered out, the number of "genuine" games with fully enabled profiles that managed to enter the platform's algorithmic Discovery Queue was 3,491 in 2023, and only 3,874 in 2024. Looking at the trend over the last five years, while the gross release volume has more than doubled, the net commercial indie game volume has remained stable in the 3,500 to 4,500 range annually, proving that the competitive arena has not expanded as much as assumed.

## The Impact of AI Disclosure Policies on the Market

The biggest catalyst behind this disproportionate surge in release volumes is generative AI tools, which have dramatically reduced production planning, coding, and asset creation costs—by roughly four orders of magnitude. This technological shift forced Valve to implement a mandatory "AI Content Disclosure" policy for every new game uploaded to the platform starting in January 2024. The policy requires developers to declare their AI usage in two distinct categories: "Pre-generated" (images, code, and sounds created during development) and "Live-generated" (content generated in real-time while the game is running).

With the implementation of the disclosure policy, it became measurable how rapidly the market was flooded with AI-generated content. In 2024, 10.9% of released games declared AI usage, and this rate rose to 19.9% in 2025, covering 7,818 games. As of July 2026, 30.8% of the games released that year feature an AI declaration. Over 60% of these disclosures rely directly on visual asset generation, such as characters, backgrounds, and models. When examining player behavior, it is observed that 89% of Steam users review these disclosure tags before making a purchase decision, and a large segment remains highly skeptical of games that are purely AI products lacking human creativity. Consequently, while AI tools have inflated the gross volume by dropping entry barriers to zero, a vast majority of these games die out outside the commercial market, trapped in the "Limited Games" filter due to player disinterest.

## Revenue Distribution and Economic Polarization in the Filtered Market

Once low-effort projects and AI noise are filtered out, the revenue distribution in the remaining "real" indie game market is highly asymmetric. The macroeconomic indicators of indie games on Steam seem quite positive at first glance; in fact, indie games generated $4.4 billion in revenue in 2025, accounting for 25% of the platform's $17.7 billion annual total. In the first nine months of 2024, this share climbed as high as 48%. However, this billion-dollar volume does not represent wealth spread across the market, but rather the economic hegemony of the top 1%.

### The Earnings Curve of the Real Market and Illusions of Success

When examining the revenue distribution in the filtered market, median figures fall far below industry standards. The median gross revenue of a game released on Steam in 2025 was a mere $249; after Valve's 30% platform cut, regional pricing, and refunds are deducted, this translates to an average net earning of $174 per developer, which is less than a single day's freelance wage.

The ruthless nature of the market becomes even clearer when specific revenue brackets are analyzed. Approximately 5,700 games, which constitute the bottom 30% of the total market, failed to cross the $100 threshold. 36% of all games (~6,800 titles) hovered between $100 and $1,000, while 24% earned between $1,000 and $50,000. The share of mid-to-high segment games earning between $50,000 and $1 million is only 9.5%, whereas the "hit" games surpassing the $1 million mark make up just 0.5% of the market (about 95 games).

The argument frequently voiced in the industry regarding the "increase in the number of games earning $100,000" has also been deemed misleading by market analysts. When Valve announced at GDC 2026 that 5,863 games earned more than $100,000 in 2025, an analysis of these titles revealed that the vast majority were back-catalog products released in previous years, driving long-tail sales. According to GameDiscoverCo, only about 1,700 of those 5,863 games were new releases from 2025, corresponding to just 8.5% of the total releases that year. Furthermore, a gross revenue of $100,000 drops to roughly $70,000 net after taxes and platform cuts, a budget wholly insufficient to sustain a multi-person indie studio.

## Market Share of Low-Effort Games and Saturation by Genres

One of the most striking findings in the analysis is the total revenue generated by low-effort and AI-generated games. The approximately 5,700 games forming the bottom 30% of the market brought in a combined total revenue of only about $37 for the entire year. This conclusively proves that low-effort projects capture no share of the commercial market pie; they are an economically completely irrelevant mass that only creates "discoverability noise" on the storefront. The real market volume is dominated by the top "Triple-I" group (studios with over 50 employees earning over $50 million, producing games like Black Myth: Wukong and Baldur's Gate 3), which commands a massive 53% revenue share.

Examining pollution rates by genres and tags reveals a structural contrast. Chris Zukowski's analyses on "How To Market A Game" indicate that 2D Platformers and general Puzzle games are the most polluted genres with the highest ratio of shovelware. These genres offer the lowest entry barriers for low-effort developers, and their median revenues are close to zero. In contrast, genres demanding mechanical depth and complex AI—such as Deep Simulation, 4X Strategy, City Builders, and Roguelike Deckbuilders—cannot be easily replicated by low-quality developers and therefore hold the highest ratio of "clean" and genuine games. The high barriers in these genres result in fewer game releases but median revenue rates well above the overall market average.

## Detection Signals for Market Filtering (Methodology)

Manually reviewing over 20,000 annual releases to isolate the 4,000 competitive games is impossible. Therefore, market analysts utilize a series of proven signals and algorithmic metrics that can be applied to large datasets. These signals can be ranked and categorized from highest reliability to lowest as follows.

### Engagement and Traction Signals

These signals measure whether there is genuine marketing effort and player interest behind a project. This data is highly resistant to manipulation.

- **The 10-Review Cliff**: The Steam algorithm effectively penalizes a game with storefront invisibility until it receives at least 10 reviews from actual purchasing users (excluding free keys). Ten reviews are the key to entering Steam's "Discovery Queue". Games that fail to cross this threshold in their initial months (over 51% of the market) should be considered outside commercial competition.
- **Follower and Wishlist Correlation**: The "Follower" count is the most reliable public metric to gauge pre-launch interest. According to GameDiscoverCo's analyses, there is a proven multiplier (similar to the Boxleiter method) between followers and wishlists. In the modern Steam market, a game's wishlist count is typically about 9.6 times its follower count. Reaching the ~7,000 wishlist threshold required to enter the "Popular Upcoming" list implies a game needs at least 500-750 followers. Games below this threshold fail to gain organic visibility at launch.
- **Peak Concurrent Users (CCU) and Median Playtime**: Successfully launched games experience a clear CCU spike in their early days. Games where playtimes remain under 0.5 hours are typically shovelware products run purely for card farming.

### Store Metadata and Pricing

Because low-effort games build their revenue models on volume rather than per-unit sales, they leave obvious footprints in their pricing strategies.

- **Pricing Threshold**: Historical price trends of indie games on Steam show that games involving serious effort start at a minimum of $8 to $10. Games with no sales strategy, priced between $0.99 and $2.99 (outside of discount periods), are almost exclusively market noise.
- **Tag Manipulation and Limited Profile Flag**: Low-quality games often use irrelevant tags (e.g., adding "Open World" or "RPG" to a 2D platformer) to boost visibility. Additionally, the "Profile Features Limited" tag is mathematical proof at the platform level that a game has failed to cross a specific sales and engagement threshold, making it the most reliable filtering signal.

### Developer History and Storefront Visuals

Production speeds reach incredible levels when AI pipelines are utilized.

- **Account Release Velocity**: While a normal indie studio's production cycle ranges from one to three years, a single Steam developer account publishing more than three new games in a year, or one every month, strongly indicates an asset flip or automated AI usage pattern.
- **Store Page Sloppiness**: The absence of a video trailer containing gameplay, the use of standard Unity templates for screenshots, or a deformed main capsule art bearing generic AI artifacts serve as manual confirmation of low quality.

## A Practical Filtering Framework for Market Sizing

Using the signals analyzed above, a "Tiered Filtering Algorithm" can be designed to allow a market analyst to process a list of 20,000 annual games and isolate the approximately 4,000 commercially viable ones.

### Tier One: Hard Cutoffs

Metrics in this stage indisputably prove that the game failed to become a commercial actor. Applying these filters instantly clears about 60% of the market.

- **Rule 1**: IF the game has the "Profile Features Limited" tag → EXCLUDE.
- **Rule 2**: IF the game has "User Reviews < 10" within the first 3 months of release → EXCLUDE.
- **Rule 3**: IF the Base List Price is < $2.99 (excluding Free-to-Play and Prologues) → EXCLUDE.

### Tier Two: Scoring and Hype Thresholds

This stage eliminates projects that pass the hard cutoffs and appear "genuine," yet show absolutely no commercial traction. Analysts here apply thresholds similar to GameDiscoverCo's Hype score or VG Insights' qualifications.

- **Follower Threshold**: Games lacking 500 followers (roughly 4,800 wishlists) on launch day are filtered out. Projects that couldn't convince even a minimal audience prior to launch are commercially meaningless, regardless of the developer's effort.
- **Boxleiter Revenue Estimate**: Games with an estimated gross revenue below $10,000 in their first 30 days can be excluded from the analysis (if the research specifically targets "successful" indie games).

## False Positive Risks and Analyst Approaches

Like any filtering model, this framework carries a risk of "False Positives." A game crafted over years by a solo dev with zero marketing budget or skill might be lumped in with shovelware and eliminated simply because it couldn't surpass 10 reviews or 500 followers. Market analysts (such as Simon Carless or Chris Zukowski) acknowledge this tragic scenario but disregard these games in macroeconomic analyses. The reason is that the total revenue share of the bottom 30% of the market remains at an insignificant level, like $37. For a financial market analysis measuring commercial potential, a "work of art" that generates no revenue is statistically equal in value to an "asset flip" that generates no revenue. Therefore, industry analysts treat the data loss caused by false positives as a negligible variance when calculating net market volume.

## Data Sources and Feasibility at a Macro Scale

To apply the proposed filtering framework to 20,000 games annually, access to the right datasets and APIs is essential. The coverage, estimation accuracies, and core limitations of available data providers are as follows:

1. **Steam Web API (Official Source)**: Valve's official API is subject to a rate limit of 100,000 requests per day, which is insufficient for broad market research. Its biggest limitation is that it blocks external access to financial data, sales figures, and wishlist conversion rates, which are reserved for Steamworks partners (publishers). The official API is not optimized for bulk-pulling the "Limited Profile" data required to clean up storefront clutter.

2. **SteamDB**: This is the most comprehensive and accurate database in the market for tracking AppIDs, price histories, peak concurrent users (CCU), and especially the "Profile Features Limited" and "Steam is learning" tags. It even drills down into technical details like the ZStandard SDK. However, SteamDB does not offer a sales or revenue estimation tool, so it cannot be used in isolation to calculate the financial size of the market.

3. **Gamalytic**: It is highly successful at analyzing the depths of the indie market, particularly small projects generating under $10,000. Gamalytic's innovation is surpassing the old "Boxleiter" method in sales estimation; it runs regression analyses on numerous variables, including the release year (accounting for the 2019 review algorithm change), review scores, and median playtime. Tests have shown that 77% of Gamalytic's estimates fall within a 30% margin of error of actual sales figures. Furthermore, its ability to analyze player overlaps provides unique capabilities for measuring genre saturation.

4. **VG Insights**: This is the standard tool for analyzing "Triple-I" (high-budget indie) projects, macro market trends, and regional revenue differences. VG Insights also uses an "Upgraded Boxleiter" multiplier, but its formula is calibrated with specific weights by genre and release year. Its primary weakness is a tendency to overestimate revenues for heavily discounted games, as its estimation algorithms cannot directly see discount periods, refunds, or in-game purchases.

5. **SteamSpy**: Although historically the industry's first major data provider, it largely lost its accuracy following Valve's 2018 privacy policy changes (making user profiles private by default). Today, it lags behind the statistical regression models employed by Gamalytic and VG Insights.

## Comprehensive Analysis Strategy

For an organization seeking to analyze a market of 20,000+ games annually, the optimal feasibility approach is: immediately detecting and clearing "Limited Profile" tags and sub-$3 games via SteamDB to clean the database; exporting the remaining competitive set of roughly 4,000 games to Gamalytic for player overlap and launch traction analysis; and finally, cross-verifying genre-based revenue projections and Triple-I / AA classifications using VG Insights reports.

The actual indie game market exists in a much smaller arena than it appears, governed by much harsher rules and increasingly narrowing economic margins (with revenues shifting heavily to the top 1%). Purging algorithmically filtered market noise from the data is unquestionably the first rule to understanding the true financial climate of the gaming industry.
